Essay · September 2026

The Decomposition Fallacy

12 min read

On Monday, Cognition closed a $2 billion Series E at a $48 billion valuation. The company’s pitch is that its coding agent, Devin, lets engineers “operate more like architects and delegate execution to swarms of agents”. This represents another change in software engineering as a profession - one that famously started out as clerical, mechanical work before shifting towards the ‘forward deployed’ model we see today.

The software industry will be fine. Agents won’t make software obsolete, since they will always need somewhere to read from and write to. But a lot of anxiety is surrounding the labor that has been underpinning software companies this entire time; who is currently doing the work that agents are also capable of doing. So the question isn’t whether software will be fine, but rather who becomes the new architects, and who doesn’t end up anywhere at all.

A brief history lesson: the word ‘agent’ has meant several things in the last four years. In the spring of 2023, it meant AutoGPT: a loop that fed a language model its own output and asked it to keep going until a goal was reached. These ended up being ineffectual, and the hype around them died off within a year. But as it turns out, agents are great for coding. Code is the one domain where an agent’s output can be checked mechanically, i.e. the code works or it doesn’t, and that gives the model a feedback loop to iterate on. GitHub Copilot had been autocompleting lines since 2021, but it was more of a suggestion engine than an autonomous coder.

The shift came in March 2024, when Cognition demoed Devin as “the first AI software engineer” and posted a 13.9% score on SWE-bench, a benchmark of real-life issues that the agent has to resolve from end-to-end. Within eighteen months the frontier labs were scoring over 70% on the human-validated version of the same benchmark. Anthropic shipped Claude Code in early 2025, OpenAI followed with Codex, and by October 2025 Anthropic was saying that many of their teams were writing code with their own models.

Again, coding is where agents were successfully implemented first because coding is where agents can be told they're right or wrong. Everything else, from customer support to compliance to sales operations, has a slower, noisier feedback loop and requires much more finesse. Enterprise ‘agent platforms’ from the incumbent software vendors started shipping in late 2024 and 2025, and the launch of several more in early 2026 is what set off the repricing of the software sector.

This is why software labor is the right place to look. Software engineers are, in many ways, the first knowledge workers to have a capable agent pointed at their work, and their employers are the companies with the most incentive (and ability) to use it. So how is automation changing this profession, exactly?

The standard framework for understanding how automatable a job is, is to break it apart into a set of tasks and see which ones a machine is capable of handling - Charles Babbage first employed it in the 19th century, analyzing factory work across England and the rest of Europe. This is how automation forecasts are built, and this technique is still employed in the literature today. But, it has been proven fallible.

In 2013, Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne produced their landmark estimate that 47% of American jobs were at high risk of computerization. The occupation that Frey & Osborne saw as ‘most at risk’ was insurance underwriting. Between 2013 and 2021, employment in insurance underwriting grew 16.4%. The occupation they rated safest, recreational therapy, shrank 8.9%. A 2022 retrospective by the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation found the overall correlation between predicted automation risk and actual job loss was 0.26 - hardly notable at all.

This isn’t because breaking jobs into tasks is the wrong approach, per se. Decomposition can be valuable in understanding complex systems. The thing that we should be wary of is assuming that all of the parts are independent; that automating Task A doesn’t change how Tasks B, C, D, etc. are performed. Consider what actually happened with insurance underwriting. Many of the routine tasks, such as data collection and risk scoring, were automated. But rather than eliminating underwriters, it freed them to concentrate on more complex cases, and on relationship management. The industry saw growth because the human aspect became more valuable, not less.

In late 2025, Joshua S. Gans and Avi Goldfarb introduced the O-ring model as an alternative framework for labor automation, especially in terms of knowledge work. “O-Ring Automation” builds on a 1993 production function introduced by Michael Kremer (originally used in the context of development economics). The crux of the argument is that in many production processes, the overall output depends on the quality of each component, and a weakness in one of them can undermine the quality of the whole. Formally, this means the final output is the product of all task qualities multiplied together rather than their sum. As a result, improving one task will magnify the value of the other tasks related to it.

Gans & Goldfarb construct a deliberately stripped-down model of work to isolate the consequences of task interdependence. Within their parameters, a worker has a fixed amount of time to allocate across the tasks that make up a job; machines can replace individual tasks at a given quality level and a given cost; and when a task is automated, the worker reallocates their freed-up time to concentrate on their remaining tasks. This extra time also allows them to increase the quality of their work. Together, this makes up the ‘focus effect’, or the focus mechanism, that forms the crux of Gans & Goldfarb’s rationale.

Take a mid-level engineer on a platform team at a SaaS company. Her week is some mix of writing boilerplate, writing tests, reviewing other people’s code, triaging incidents, making architecture calls, and sitting in on customer conversations to figure out what people are asking for. An agent now writes the boilerplate and the first pass at the tests. In a decomposition framework, that’s two of six tasks gone, or a third of the job being considered ‘automatable’. In the O-ring framework, the automation actually begins a magnification of her role.

First, her remaining tasks get better. She has more time - and headspace - for code review and incident triage, which means that these get tackled with a clearer head. As a side effect, she raises the standards for these tasks as well. Suppose the firm is now considering an agent for code review. The relevant comparison is not against the engineer’s old reviews that she was doing while juggling multiple tasks, but against the engineer’s new reviews that had more time allocated towards them. This is a much harder target, and Gans & Goldfarb say as much: the remaining manual tasks get “performed with disproportionately high care” and become “disproportionately hard to replace”.

Also note that automation tends to arrive in bundles rather than on a task-by-task basis. The return on automating any given task depends on how many are already automated, so it can be a loss to automate one task in isolation and a gain to automate three together. In our engineer’s case, taking away the responsibility of writing boilerplate code alone may not pay off if she still has to context-switch into the codebase to check it. On the other hand, taking away boilerplate, tests, and first-pass review as a package might. This is why organizations that look like they’re ‘falling behind’ with AI can adopt it and reorganize abruptly, and it's a decent description of what happened to the software sector’s stock market valuation earlier this year.

Once the routine tasks are gone, what’s left in the engineer’s hands are the ‘bottleneck’ tasks; the ones that involve judgment, expertise, and social skills (at least, until AI can handle customer meetings). Because the overall output is the product of every task’s quality, the quality of those remaining tasks matters more. Gans & Goldfarb show that, under partial automation, a worker in that position can bargain for higher income as automation improves, because she controls the steps that determine the value of everything the machines just did.

There is also an emerging dimension worth noting. Agents themselves require management infrastructure: identity, permissions, and audit logging; as well as documentation of what they can/can’t do and why they carried out certain actions. We could call this the ‘system of context’ (following from the existing ‘system of record’ for the company itself), and describe it as the layer that provides legibility and transparency for agents’ actions. This system could create managerial tasks for humans to perform, pushing up the value of our software engineer even more.

Now, none of this means automation can’t displace workers. It means the path from ‘this task can be automated’ to ‘this person loses their job’ is less direct than the decomposition approach lays out. The O-ring thesis only holds as long as humans are still needed in the loop (and Gans & Goldfarb are explicit about this in their conclusion: “conditional on labour remaining necessary and disagreement payoffs being fixed, improvements in automation quality can raise bargained labour income by increasing the value of the remaining bottlenecks. Once full automation becomes a credible outside option, however, that wage result becomes ambiguous”). This is true for now though, and probably will be for a while.

Agents do work. They work about 60% of the time on a single run; over eight consecutive runs, success drops to 25%. Fiddler AI and Carnegie Mellon put the overall production failure rate between 70% and 95%, depending on the task. Agents perform well on routine, context-light tasks, like drafting emails, summarizing documents, and generating code scaffolding. But they struggle in tasks requiring institutional knowledge and regulatory judgment, especially if there’s no room for error at all.

Surprisingly, Gartner predicts that 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission their autonomous agents by 2027. Shiva Varma, the firm’s senior director analyst, identified the core problem: organizations treat agents as either “fully locked down or completely trusted”, rather than calibrating the level of autonomy to fit their specific use-cases. The organizational sophistication required to deploy agents safely in high-stakes environments is itself a bottleneck, and a human one at that. So it is not just about whether or not bottleneck tasks themselves can be done by AI, but whether or not they can be done consistently well without intervention. And even that is underselling it - there needs to be a lot of trust involved in the system, and the question of whether or not we are there yet remains unanswered.

Still, none of this is permanent. Agent capabilities improve, and they scale with increased compute, fine-tuning, and better tooling. The models that power today’s agents are not the models that will power next year’s, or even next month’s. As someone who does not work at a lab or an AI company, it is impossible for me to give you a verdict, outcome, or prediction. What the O-ring theory provides (and what the production data currently supports) is that the path to full automation is substantially harder than the decomposition approach implies, precisely because the non-automatable tasks are the ones where human attention is an incredible asset. The threshold for AI accomplishing those tasks is not fixed, but capable of moving upward.

In March 2026, Anthropic researchers Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory introduced “observed exposure”, a measure combining theoretical AI capability with actual usage data from Claude’s platform. Their main finding is that AI is far from reaching its theoretical capability. The ‘Computer and Mathematical’ occupational category (which includes programmers) had 94% of its tasks rated as coverable by AI. Actual coverage on Anthropic’s platform was 33%.

More significantly, Massenkoff & McCrory found no systematic increase in unemployment for highly exposed workers since late 2022. The mass displacement that markets and policy analysts had priced in simply wasn’t materializing in the labor data. The researchers flagged the O-ring model as a possible explanation, noting that in an O-ring environment, “employment effects might be seen only when all tasks have some degree of AI penetration”. This is to say that partial automation doesn't produce partial displacement; partial automation results in a reorganization of the remaining bottleneck. Thus, the gap between theoretical AI exposure and actual labor displacement is a feature of how work responds to automation.

Three months later, Anthropic's June 2026 “Cadences” report - a survey of 9,700 users linked to platform usage data - produced what might be the most counterintuitive finding in the AI labor literature so far. The users who delegate most aggressively to AI report the most optimistic views about AI’s impact on their pay, job security, and ability to find new work. 86% of respondents reported speed improvements; 82% reported expanding the scope of what they could accomplish; 69% said quality had improved. 57% said AI had made their skills more valuable; 68% reported learning more with AI. The people using AI most intensively aren’t experiencing deskilling. Rather, they are finding that their work has become harder, more interesting, and more valuable since the implementation of AI. Experienced workers, when asked what AI couldn’t replace, cited judgment, contextual awareness, and situational reasoning, reflecting the hard-to-automate bottleneck tasks implied by the O-ring model.

The data also tells us something less comforting. In the Cadences report, over a third of respondents expected junior colleagues to face a high probability of job loss within a year, and first-year workers said AI could handle about 10 percentage points more of their work than workers with fifteen or more years of experience. In August, Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen’s “Canaries in the Coal Mine” study was updated using ADP payroll records. Employment for 22-25 year olds in highly AI-exposed roles is now 19% lower than what it would be if it had matched the growth of less-exposed roles - up from 15% a year ago. Experienced workers in the same occupations do not have this gap: their employment is flat or, in some cases, rising.

This split is what the O-ring model predicts almost exactly, which should make us both more confident in the framework and more worried about what it leaves out: the mechanism by which people are able to gain the judgment and specialized knowledge needed to successfully work with AI systems. (I wrote about the bleakness of the youth job market last year - the unemployment rate for 16-24 year olds jumped from 8.5% when I wrote the piece to 9.1% in July 2026, which should give you a sense of how punishing it is on the ground for young people and recent graduates)

So: software as an industry is safe. Software labor is reorganizing toward judgment, contextual reasoning, and interpersonal and institutional knowledge. Granted, the data is early, but many sources point in the same direction: the workers most embedded in AI workflows are the ones reporting that their remaining work feels more valuable, not less. Moreover, the absence of displacement in the most exposed occupations demands an explanation. As Massenkoff and McCrory argue, if tasks are interdependent, displacement won’t occur until the entire bundle of tasks is automatable. What will show up in companies before that is reorganization, which is a different matter entirely.

What the data can’t tell us yet is how the distribution will play out, i.e. how many workers end up on the valuable side of the bottleneck and how many end up having their work automated away. The concern about entry-level and junior workers is very real and should not be handwaved away. If software labor reorganizes around judgment and institutional knowledge, there still needs to be a pipeline for younger workers to develop those traits - there are no shortcuts around it.

The decompositional approach was to assume that automating a task removes that task and nothing else. We know now that the remaining work gains value, and the people doing it become harder to replace. The mistake that needs to be avoided is assuming that those people will keep appearing on their own. Nobody is born an architect, after all.
